# Argument map

The Article's argument Part by Part: each claim, the authority it rests on, the strongest objection to it and the
response. Authorities are cited in full in the notes of the
[Article](../manuscript/who-may-choose-the-lesser-risk.md).

## Thesis

Risk-risk analysis supports a **comparative justification duty** and does not substitute for authorization.
Sustained cooling also calls for a **continuity duty**, under which political control can change without forcing an
abrupt physical stop. The first duty assigns the authority to decide, and the second makes a withdrawal of consent
something an institution can survive. Together they support governed research and restraint on deployment until
comparison and continuity are institutionally credible, with continuing scrutiny of the harms of nondeployment.

## Part I. The authorization problem

| Claim | Authority | Objection | Response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Identifying competing risks cannot say who may choose among them | The structure of stratospheric aerosol injection: people cannot escape the resulting climate | Risk-risk analysis already decides | It identifies what a comparison must include and leaves the authority to decide unassigned |
| The Article answers institutionally a question three literatures leave open | Risk-risk analysis; the literature on termination; Szerszynski et al. and Horton et al. on democracy | Solar geoengineering and democratic control cannot be reconciled | The comparative justification duty assigns the authority, and the continuity duty lets consent be withdrawn safely |

## Part II. What risk-risk analysis can establish

| Claim | Authority | Objection | Response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The framework already includes magnitude, uncertainty, timing, distribution and alternatives | Graham and Wiener; Felgenhauer et al. | It ignores justice | The gap is about who decides, and not about what is counted |
| Two of its categories are already positive law | UNCLOS art. 195; ITLOS Advisory Opinion (2024) ¶ 231 | Transfer and transformation are analytic labels | Article 195 prohibits transferring damage from one area to another or transforming one type of pollution into another. It does not say who decides whether a transfer is justified |
| The categories cannot fix the unit of concern | Population and hazard definitions change the label | Use the categories to decide | They organize the inquiry |
| Pathways interact and can be counted twice | The IPCC's assessment of the "slippery slope" | List every harm separately | Dependencies are disclosed along with harms |
| Omitted people differ from omitted impacts | Wiener, *Disregard and Due Regard*; Flegal and Gupta | Better projections solve equity | People represented in a model need a route to challenge its comparator and its measures |

## Part III. Comparing policies without choosing the answer in advance

| Claim | Authority | Objection | Response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Three baselines are kept apart | A reference climate; the future without the intervention; feasible portfolios | One counterfactual is enough | Confusing them turns a sound result into an unsupported policy conclusion |
| The analysis has two stages: marginal physical effects, then whole portfolios | Emissions and adaptation held fixed, then allowed to vary | One stage suffices | Each answers a question the other cannot |
| Feasibility stays contestable | Sponsor capacity against adaptation finance | Past failure shows what is feasible | Past failure informs estimates and is no law of nature |
| Favorable evidence is real and limited | Irvine and Keith; Proctor et al.; Fan et al.; the WMO ozone assessment | Models show net benefit | Aggregate moderation is not individual improvement |
| Uncertainty does not settle values | The separation of model results, disputed assumptions, unquantified hazards and evaluative choices | More data will resolve the dispute | No observation determines acceptable loss or who may impose it |

## Part IV. Research permission and the limits of existing law

| Instrument | Supplies | Leaves open |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The line between research and deployment | Scrutiny by expected effects and cumulative scale | A universal mass threshold |
| National Academies (2021); OSTP (2023) | Recommendations for governed research | Legal authorization |
| Paris Agreement | Temperature, emissions, adaptation, equity, finance | A permitting body |
| CBD Decision X/33 ¶ 8(w) | Guidance for restraint with a research exception | A binding prohibition |
| *Pulp Mills*; *Certain Activities* | Environmental impact assessment, notification and consultation for significant transboundary risk | A general consultation right for affected populations |
| ICJ Advisory Opinion on climate change (2025) | Stringent due diligence in preventing significant harm to the climate system | An adjudicated conclusion about deployment |
| Vienna Convention art. 2 | A duty to protect against activities likely to modify the ozone layer | An authority able to refuse |
| UNFCCC art. 4(1)(f) | Impact assessment of measures taken to mitigate or adapt | The same |
| ILC Guidelines on the Protection of the Atmosphere, guideline 7 | Intentional large-scale modification only with prudence and caution, in a commentary that names stratospheric aerosols | The same |
| ILC Prevention Articles arts. 9, 10 | An equitable balance of interests that includes alternatives | If consultations fail, the state of origin may still authorize. The comparative justification duty departs from existing law at exactly this point |
| ENMOD | Prohibition of hostile environmental modification | Peaceful intervention |
| Fla. Stat. § 403.411 (2025) | A prohibition within one territory | Deployment anywhere else, which is where the authorization problem lives |

## Part V. A comparative justification duty

| Element | Content | Objection | Response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who must justify | The deploying state or coalition, and operators through domestic authorization | Why burden the intervener and not emitters? | The duty attaches to decisions about operating a controllable program; emitters remain accountable, and restraint is also explained |
| What | A public comparison of a specified strategy with feasible alternatives, disaggregated by exposure, with disclosed weights and answers to the strongest objections | Research permission implies approval | It creates no presumption |
| Burden | An ex ante regulatory record | *Pulp Mills* rejects reversing the burden of proof | The justification is preventive and does not reverse a burden in litigation |
| Making challenge consequential | Funded participation, designated representation of future and ecological interests, a review council with an independent science panel, and separate concurrence of sponsors and exposed constituencies | Consultation suffices | Exposed representatives who are not sponsors can collectively withhold approval or require redesign |
| Separate concurrence has precedents | Montreal Protocol art. 10(9): a two-thirds majority that includes a majority of each group of parties. 11 U.S.C. §§ 1126(c), 1129: acceptance class by class, with a floor for each dissenter | A supermajority is enough (Weitzman) | Separate concurrence adds that the majority includes those who would bear the exposure |
| Responsibility under uncertainty | Monitoring, adaptation, emergency response and claims funded in advance, with liability kept separate from assistance | Pay only after attribution | Attribution may be impractical. Horton and Keith propose parametric insurance for a related reason; here pre-funded assistance is a condition of authorization |
| Priority rule | A strong presumption against grave, concentrated increases in harm relative to the defended comparator | Aggregate net benefit governs | The rule is a floor defined against a comparator, as the liquidation floor is in reorganization law. Many modest benefits do not settle a conflict over subsistence or survival |

## Part VI. The problem of exit

| Claim | Authority | Objection | Response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Termination risk depends on conditions | Parker and Irvine | Catastrophe is inevitable | Gradual withdrawal, limited cooling and redundancy change the risk |
| The exit paradox | Expiry of an authorization after sustained cooling could force abrupt cessation | Periodic review keeps permission conditional | Only when political exit and physical exit are separated |
| An abrupt lawful termination has already happened | The 2020 sulfur limit for marine fuel (MEPC.280(70)); Yuan et al. | The paradox is hypothetical | A lawful regulatory decision ended a cooling influence abruptly and planned no climatic trajectory |
| Governance, operation and trajectory are separate decisions | A bounded interim successor; a tapering pathway | Succession renews the rejected operator's mandate | The transition authority is agreed before deployment, bounded and independently reviewed |
| The law already has instruments for systems that cannot safely stop | 12 U.S.C. § 5365(d)(1) (resolution plans); 10 C.F.R. § 50.75(a) (decommissioning assurance); 15 U.S.C. § 717f(b) (abandonment by permission); 12 U.S.C. § 1821(n) (bridge institutions) | Continuity is a novel demand | In order: a continuity plan filed before operation, financing committed before it is needed, an exit that requires permission, and an interim successor with a bounded mandate |
| The continuity duty reconciles the two duties | Parker and Irvine; Ricke, Moreno-Cruz and Caldeira | Wider control raises the chance that an authorization lapses | Under the continuity duty a refusal changes who operates and starts a reviewed taper |
| Continuity is an obligation before deployment and it has a cost | Redundancy, successor access, monitoring, transition finance | Starting is cheap | The ability to start is not the ability to discharge the obligations |

## Part VII. Objections and the limits of the argument

| Objection | Response |
| --- | --- |
| **Restraint privileges a damaging status quo** | Restraining institutions say what would change their decision, provide alternative protection and review on schedule. The preference for intervention is documented: a survey of more than thirty thousand respondents in thirty countries found publics in the Global South more supportive than publics in the Global North (Baum et al.). The harms of nondeployment still do not let any willing sponsor decide for everyone |
| **Research itself should be barred** (Biermann et al.) | Inquiry is permitted when its informational value, alternatives, direct effects and contribution to a program withstand review, and reasons against deployment are funded too |
| **Transfer to outer space** | The duties to compare and to answer objections travel. The continuity architecture needs the same dependence on continued operation |

## Conclusion

A deployment proponent defends its comparator, exposes its distributional assumptions to challenge, and
establishes responsibility for both continued operation and eventual withdrawal. The law already knows how to keep
an indispensable system running while control over it changes hands, and the continuity duty asks that the same be
arranged for the stratosphere before anyone depends on it.
